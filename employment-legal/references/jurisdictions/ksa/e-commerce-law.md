## Scope

The Law regulates "electronic commerce": an economic activity carried on by a service provider and a consumer, wholly or partly by electronic means, to sell products, provide services, advertise them or exchange their data (Art. 1). It binds service providers in the Kingdom, practitioners outside the Kingdom who offer products or services accessible to consumers in the Kingdom, and consumers (Art. 2). Its obligations are consumer-protection obligations: identity and pre-contract disclosure, invoices, advertising content, a seven-day withdrawal right, late-delivery rescission, personal-data limits, and registration of the online store. "Consumer" is defined as the person who deals in e-commerce wishing to obtain the products or services (Art. 1) without limiting it to natural persons or non-business purposes, so a business customer buying online is not excluded by the words of the definition; in practice the Law is applied and enforced as a B2C instrument and rarely governs negotiated B2B contracts (MSAs, SaaS subscriptions negotiated between companies) `[model knowledge — verify]`. Matters it does not address are governed by the Electronic Transactions Law and other laws (Art. 24). It is not a data-protection law; the PDPL governs personal data generally (see `personal-data-protection-law.md`).

## Open questions for a local practitioner

- The Implementing Regulation (Art. 25) was not read. It carries the error-correction window (Art. 4), the categories of personal data to be kept private (Art. 5), the additional disclosure and pre-contract data (Art. 6(d), 7(g)), invoice details (Art. 8), additional withdrawal-right exclusions (Art. 13(2)(g)), and the controls for registering an online store on the CR (Art. 15).
- Whether the Ministry or the courts treat business customers as "consumers" under Art. 1; the text does not exclude them, but the Law is enforced as a B2C instrument in practice.
- How Art. 5 (consent for disclosure, retention limited to the transaction) is reconciled with the PDPL's legitimate-interest basis and retention rules after 2023; which prevails for a Saudi consumer-facing platform.
- The Ministry's rules under Art. 16 for intermediary platforms and store-authentication bodies (Maroof) and whether registration there substitutes for the Art. 6(a) address disclosure in practice.
- Whether the seven-day withdrawal right is treated as available for a subscription service where the consumer has accessed the service within the seven days.

## How skills should use this file

- `review` (router): load only when the agreement is consumer-facing online terms, a marketplace or platform agreement, or a vendor whose business is online retail to Saudi consumers. For negotiated B2B MSAs, SaaS subscriptions and NDAs this file is not the governing instrument; the router should cite Art. 1 (definition of consumer) and the Scope note when explaining why it is not applied, and flag the B2B point as `[model knowledge — verify]`.
- `saas-msa-review`, `vendor-agreement-review` (commercial-legal): when the vendor sells to Saudi consumers through the customer's platform, or the customer is a marketplace, cite Art. 5 (consumer personal data), Art. 10-11 (advertising binds), Art. 13-14 (withdrawal and late delivery) as pass-through obligations, and Art. 18 for the fine ceiling.
- `diligence-issue-extraction`, `tabular-review`, `entity-compliance` (corporate-legal): for a target that sells online, cite Art. 6 (store disclosures), Art. 15 (online store on the CR) and Art. 17-18 (blocking exposure) as compliance findings.
- `stakeholder-summary`, `escalation-flagger`: cite Art. 18 (SAR 1,000,000 and blocking) when quantifying exposure.
- Stop rule: if a skill needs a rule that is missing from this table, depends on the Regulation, or is tagged `[model knowledge — verify]` (the B2B exclusion, Maroof, the PDPL interaction), it must say so and stop or flag for Saudi counsel; it must not extend this Law to a B2B contract or exclude it from one on its own reasoning.
